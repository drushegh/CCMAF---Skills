# Character and voice

## The working parts of a character

| Part | Question | Shows up as |
|---|---|---|
| Want | What do they consciously pursue in this story? | Goals, plans, the actions that drive scenes |
| Need | What must change in them for a satisfying ending (which they may not get)? | The arc; what the climax tests |
| Wound or false belief | What happened, or what do they believe, that keeps want and need apart? | Blind spots, repeated mistakes, overreactions |
| Contradiction | Where do they act against type? | The moments readers remember |
| Cost | What will they give up for the want? | Stakes made personal |
| Specificity | What do they notice, own, avoid, say? | Concrete detail in their scenes and their dialogue |

Not every story needs all of these. Flash fiction may carry one want and one
contradiction. A novel's protagonist usually needs all six, and its main
antagonist at least want, cost and a reason they believe they are right.

## Character is revealed through choice

- What a character chooses under pressure tells the reader more than any
  description. Low-stakes choices establish habit; high-stakes choices
  reveal who they are when habit fails.
- Diagnostic for a flat character: list the last five choices they made on
  the page. If most are reactions, or obvious, the character is passive or
  predictable. Add a choice with a real cost, or one the reader would not
  make.
- Backstory earns its place only when it explains a present choice the
  reader is already curious about. Delivered before that curiosity, it is
  a lecture.

## Ensemble and secondary characters

- Each recurring character should want something in their scenes, even if
  it is small (to leave, to be believed, to win the argument).
- Two characters with the same function (two loyal friends, two gruff
  mentors) should be merged or differentiated.
- Name test: distinct first letters and syllable counts for major
  characters reduce reader confusion. Avoid stock generated names (see
  `revision-passes.md`, "Generated-fiction tells").

## Point of view and psychic distance

| POV | Gives | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| First person | Intimacy, voice, unreliability | Narrator knowing what they cannot know; every sentence starting "I" |
| Close third | Intimacy with flexibility; easy to switch POV characters at breaks | Head-hopping mid-scene |
| Distant or omniscient third | Range, irony, a narrating personality | Losing emotional connection; needs a confident narrator |
| Second person | Strangeness, implication | Fatigue at length; works best in short forms |
| Multiple POV | Scope, dramatic irony | Each POV must earn its chapters; distinct voices |

**Psychic distance** runs from far ("The ferry crossed twice a day that
autumn, and few people used it.") through middle ("Nora disliked the
ferry.") to deep ("Diesel and wet rope again. Twenty minutes of this."). Move along it deliberately: pull back for
transitions and summary, push in for emotional peaks. Unplanned jumps feel
like a wobble in the camera.

**Filter words** (she saw, he felt, she noticed, he realised) add distance.
In deep POV, cut the filter and give the perception directly. Keep them
where the act of noticing matters.

## Voice

Voice is the sum of choices: diction (plain or ornate, period or modern,
dialect), sentence rhythm, what the narrator notices and ignores, attitude
(wry, earnest, cold), and what is left unsaid.

When matching a writer's voice (editor, co-writer and ghost modes):

1. **Read at least two thousand words** of their prose first, if it exists.
2. **Profile it** in a few lines: typical sentence length and variation,
   paragraph length, dialogue tag habits, punctuation habits, register,
   metaphor density and source (nature, machinery, food), humour, how
   interiority is rendered.
3. **Draft a short passage** and set it beside theirs. Read both aloud. If
   yours is smoother, more balanced, more explanatory or more ornate, it
   will stand out. Roughen it to match.
4. **Do not "improve" their signature habits** (long sentences, comma
   splices used for rhythm, present tense, dialect spellings) without
   asking.

## Brief questions (ghost and co-writer modes)

Ask, or state assumptions for, the ones that are unclear:

- Form and length; audience age range; genre and comparable titles the
  writer likes (as a feel reference, not to imitate text).
- POV and tense.
- Protagonist's want, the central conflict, and how the writer sees the
  ending (or whether to leave it open).
- Tone (dark, comic, cosy, lyrical) and content limits (violence, sex,
  language) for this audience.
- Setting specifics the writer cares about, and anything that must or must
  not happen.
- Whether this is for practice, for submission, or private.

## Exercises (coach mode)

- **Choice ladder:** write the protagonist facing three choices of rising
  cost on the same day.
- **Same scene, two distances:** write one paragraph far, then deep.
- **Voice swap:** rewrite a page of dialogue so each line could only be
  spoken by its character; then remove the tags and check it still works.
- **Object biography:** a character described only through three things
  they own and how they treat them.
