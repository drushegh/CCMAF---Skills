# Structure and plot

Structures are lenses for diagnosis, not moulds. Use them to find where a
draft loses tension, never to force a story into beats it does not need.

## The central question

Every story raises a question the reader wants answered (will she get out,
who did it, will they end up together, what is wrong with this house) and
answers it at the climax. Diagnostics:

- Can you state it in one sentence? If not, the story may not have one yet,
  or it has several competing.
- When is it raised? Later than the first chapter (or first page in short
  fiction) and the opening drifts.
- Is it answered? An ambiguous ending can work; an unanswered question
  usually feels like a lost thread.
- Subplots raise their own smaller questions and should resolve before or
  at the main climax, ideally pressing on it.

## Turning points

A turning point changes the situation so the character cannot go back to
the previous plan. Most working structures agree on a handful:

| Point | Roughly where (novel) | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Inciting incident | First 10 to 15% | Disrupts the status quo; raises the central question |
| First commitment | About 25% | Protagonist chooses to pursue the want; no going back |
| Midpoint | About 50% | A revelation or reversal that changes the nature of the conflict (reactive to active, or the goal itself shifts) |
| Crisis or low point | About 75% | The plan fails; the false belief is tested |
| Climax | Final 10 to 15% | The central question answered through the protagonist's choice |
| Resolution | After the climax | The new normal; brief |

Percentages are loose guides. Short stories compress to: situation, a
complication, a turn, a resolution or revelation, often in a few scenes.

## Act structures as lenses

- **Three acts:** set-up, confrontation, resolution. Useful for spotting a
  middle that does not escalate.
- **Four parts** (set-up, response, attack, resolution): splits the middle
  at the midpoint, so the protagonist moves from reacting to acting.
- **Kishotenketsu:** introduction, development, twist, reconciliation.
  Tension from juxtaposition rather than conflict; good lens for quiet
  literary pieces.
- **Try-fail cycles:** the protagonist attempts, fails or succeeds at a
  cost, and the situation worsens. Each cycle should raise the stakes or
  change the approach.
- **Mystery structure:** the crime, the investigation through clues and red
  herrings, the false solution, the true solution. Fair play: every clue the
  solution needs is on the page before the reveal.

## Pacing

Pacing is the ratio of time on the page to time in the story, and of
tension to relief.

- **Too slow:** long summaries of things the reader can infer, repeated
  conflict at the same level, description that does not characterise, too
  many sequel beats in a row.
- **Too fast:** turning points without the reaction that lets them land,
  stakes never felt, a climax resolved in a paragraph.
- Diagnostic: mark each chapter or scene with its tension level (1 to 5)
  and plot the numbers. A flat line or a long dip in the middle is visible
  at once.

## Openings

- Start where the pressure begins, with a character doing something in a
  specific place. The reader should meet a voice, a person and a hint of
  the central question quickly.
- Common weak openings: waking up, weather, a dream, a mirror description,
  a page of backstory, a prologue that the story would work without, an
  action scene with no one the reader cares about yet.
- A good test: if chapter one were cut, would anything important be lost?
  Many first drafts start a chapter too early.

## Endings

- The climax should turn on the protagonist's choice, not on luck or a
  rescue. Endings that resolve the external plot without testing the
  internal arc feel hollow.
- Earned, not tidy: avoid resolving every relationship, stating the moral,
  or a final paragraph that explains what it all meant.
- Echo the opening where natural; an image or line that returns changed
  shows how far the story has moved.

## Outlining versus discovery drafting

Both are legitimate. Help the writer with the method they use:

- **Outliners:** a one-line-per-scene outline with each scene's goal,
  conflict and outcome; check the turning points exist before drafting.
- **Discovery writers:** draft freely, then reverse-outline
  (`revision-passes.md`) to find the structure that emerged and the gaps.
- **Hybrid:** know the ending and a few signposts; discover the rest.

## Series and serials

- Each instalment needs its own question answered, plus a series question
  carried forward.
- Keep a series bible (characters, places, timeline, rules of the world) in
  the workspace; `editorial-review` uses it for consistency checks.
