# Scene and dialogue

## What a scene must do

A scene is a unit of change. Something is different at the end: a value has
turned (hope to despair, ignorance to knowledge, ally to enemy). If nothing
turns, the scene is summary or set-up and should be cut, compressed or
merged with one that does turn.

Scene card (use for planning and for diagnosis):

```text
POV:        whose scene
Goal:       what they want in this scene
Conflict:   what stands in the way (a person, a fact, themselves)
Turn:       the value at the start -> the value at the end
Outcome:    yes, no, yes-but, no-and
New info:   what the reader learns
```

"Yes-but" and "no-and" outcomes keep a story moving; a clean "yes" ends
tension, so it is saved for the end of a line of action.

## Scene and sequel

- **Scene:** goal, conflict, disaster (the outcome).
- **Sequel:** reaction (emotion), dilemma (options), decision (the next
  goal).

Sequels can be a paragraph or a chapter. Too many skipped and the
character feels like a machine; too many lingered on and pace dies. After a
major turning point, give at least a beat of reaction.

## Enter late, leave early

- Begin after the small talk, the arrival, the "hello"; begin when the
  conflict is about to engage.
- End once the turn has happened. A paragraph of reflection after the turn
  usually deflates it. (Ending every scene on a reflective line is also a
  generated-fiction tell.)

## Scene versus summary

| Use scene for | Use summary for |
|---|---|
| Turning points, confrontations, revelations | Travel, routine, passing time |
| Moments the reader must experience | Events the reader needs to know but not live |
| Relationships changing | Repetition of something already dramatised |

"Show, don't tell" is shorthand for this choice, not an absolute. Telling
is efficient and right for transitions and for emotions already earned.
The note is "this moment needs to be a scene", not "never tell".

## Dialogue

What good dialogue does: reveals character, advances conflict, carries
subtext, and sounds like speech without transcribing it.

- **Each line wants something.** People talk to get things: reassurance,
  information, the upper hand, to be left alone.
- **People rarely answer the question asked.** Evasion, deflection and
  answering a different question create subtext.
- **Cut the pleasantries,** the names in every line, and the full
  sentences people do not use.
- **Distinct voices:** vocabulary, sentence length, what each character
  avoids saying. Test: remove the tags; can you tell who speaks?
- **Dialect and accent:** convey with word choice and syntax rather than
  heavy phonetic spelling, which tires readers and can caricature. The
  writer decides; flag only if readability suffers.

## Subtext

Subtext is the gap between what a character says and what they mean, and
the reader's pleasure is in closing it. It disappears when:

- the character states the feeling outright ("I'm angry because you
  forgot");
- the narration explains the line that has just shown it ("she said,
  hurt that he had forgotten");
- another character translates it.

Fix: cut the explanation; give a concrete action or a deflecting line
instead; trust the reader.

## Tags and beats

- "Said" and "asked" are nearly invisible; use them. Fancy tags
  (exclaimed, retorted, hissed) draw attention and often tell the emotion
  the line should show.
- Adverbs on tags ("she said angrily") usually mean the line is not doing
  its job.
- **Action beats** ("She set the cup down. 'Fine.'") identify the speaker
  and add physical life. Overused, every line gets a gesture and the scene
  becomes choreography.
- Punctuation conventions differ: UK publishing often uses single quotation
  marks, US double. Follow the writer's or the publisher's style sheet;
  `editorial-review` checks consistency.

## Exposition

- Deliver information when the reader wants it, through conflict where
  possible (an argument about the facts, a character who needs to learn
  them).
- Avoid "as you know" dialogue, where characters tell each other things
  both already know.
- Worldbuilding in speculative fiction: the reader needs enough to follow
  the stakes, not the full history. Keep the rest in the series bible.

## Description

- Select the detail that characterises (the POV character's eye) or that
  sets up later events. One precise detail beats a catalogue.
- Engage senses beyond sight, but only where they serve the moment.
- Avoid stock phrasing ("the air was thick with tension", "a smile played
  on her lips"); see the tells list in `revision-passes.md`.
