# Socratic tutoring and checking understanding

Socratic does not mean refusing to explain. It means the learner does as much
of the thinking as they can, and you supply exactly the support they need to
get there. Explain directly when the learner has no basis to reason from.

## Question types (use deliberately)

| Purpose | Example prompt |
|---|---|
| Elicit prior knowledge | "Before I explain, what do you think happens when…?" |
| Clarify | "What do you mean by 'faster' here: latency or throughput?" |
| Probe reasons | "Why does that follow? What would have to be true?" |
| Test with a case | "Does your rule still work if the list is empty?" |
| Predict | "Before we run it, what output do you expect?" |
| Contrast | "How is a mean different from a median for this data?" |
| Transfer | "Here's a situation you haven't seen. Which idea applies?" |
| Find the error | "This worked solution has one mistake. Where?" |
| Explain back | "Explain it to me as if I'd missed the lesson." |
| Metacognitive | "How confident are you, 1–5? What would raise it?" |

Ask one question at a time. Wait for the answer. Long multi-part questions
let the learner answer the easy part and skip the rest.

## Checking understanding (the alternatives to "make sense?")

- **Explain-back:** the learner states it in their own words; listen for
  missing steps and borrowed phrasing that hides confusion.
- **Apply to a new case:** different surface features, same principle.
- **Predict, then check:** commit to an answer before seeing the result.
- **Generate an example and a non-example.**
- **Spot the error** in a plausible wrong answer.
- **Teach-back of a step** in a procedure: "why this step, not that one?"

A correct answer with wrong reasoning is not mastery. Ask for the reasoning
on correct answers too, at least sometimes.

## Hint ladder (when they are stuck)

1. Re-read the question together; restate what is asked.
2. Point to the relevant idea without applying it ("think about what you
   know about rates of change").
3. Narrow the step ("what's the first thing you'd compute?").
4. Give a partial worked step and let them finish.
5. Show the full solution, then give a near-identical problem to do alone.

Move down the ladder only when the previous rung has not worked. Note which
rung was needed; it tells you what to review.

## Feedback on errors

- Be specific and immediate: what was wrong, why, and the correct version.
- Separate the error from the person: "this step assumes X" rather than
  "you got it wrong".
- Re-ask the same item later in the session and again at the next session;
  a corrected error needs retrieval too.
- Do not praise vaguely. "Good, you checked the units before combining" is
  useful; "great!" is not.

## Misconception repair

1. **Surface it:** ask a question whose answer reveals the belief.
2. **Make the belief explicit:** "so you're expecting heavier things to fall
   faster?"
3. **Create conflict:** a case where the belief predicts wrongly, ideally one
   they can check.
4. **Offer the better model** and show it handles both the old cases and the
   conflicting one.
5. **Revisit later**: misconceptions return. Put a card or a question on the
   schedule specifically for it.

## Self-explanation prompts

While working through a worked example or a text, ask the learner to say why
each step is taken or how a sentence connects to what came before. Useful
when they have enough prior knowledge to generate explanations; if they
cannot, give the explanation and ask them to restate it.

## Pacing and tone

- Short turns. A coaching turn is usually a few sentences and a question.
- Normalise difficulty; do not catastrophise errors.
- Adjust to the learner: an expert wants brisk challenge, a nervous novice
  wants smaller steps and more success early.
- If they ask for the answer outright, give it when appropriate; then check
  they can reproduce the reasoning.

## Pitfalls

- Leading questions that contain the answer ("so it's X, right?").
- Socratic theatre: questioning when the learner has nothing to reason from.
- Correcting without asking again later.
- Assuming silence or "OK" means understanding.
- Teaching something false confidently. When unsure, say so and check
  (`read-the-damn-docs`, `academic-research`).
