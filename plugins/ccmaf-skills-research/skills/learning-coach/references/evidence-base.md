# The evidence base, and its limits

Use this to choose techniques and to answer "does this actually work?"
honestly. The summary below reflects widely cited reviews of the learning
literature. Where you need to cite a specific study for a learner or a
document, find and verify it through `academic-research`; do not quote
effect sizes from memory.

## Strength-of-evidence ladder

| Technique | What it is | Evidence | Conditions and caveats |
|---|---|---|---|
| Retrieval practice (practice testing) | Producing answers from memory: questions, flashcards, blank-page recall, past papers | Strong and broad, across ages, subjects and lab and classroom settings | Needs feedback when answers are wrong; benefits show most on delayed tests; low-stakes, not anxiety-inducing |
| Spaced (distributed) practice | Spreading study of the same material across sessions | Strong and broad | Optimal gaps depend on how long you need to retain; cramming can win on tomorrow's test and lose a month later |
| Interleaving | Mixing problem types or categories within a session | Good for discrimination tasks (maths problem types, categorising examples) | Less clear for very different topics; feels harder and learners often rate it worse while it works better |
| Worked examples, then fading | Studying solved problems before solving, removing steps progressively | Good for novices in structured domains | Benefit shrinks or reverses as expertise grows (expertise reversal); switch to problems as competence rises |
| Elaborative interrogation and self-explanation | Asking "why is this true?" or explaining each step to yourself | Moderate | Depends on prior knowledge; weak when the learner cannot generate a correct explanation |
| Dual coding | Combining words with diagrams or visual structure | Moderate, mostly from multimedia research | Means meaningful visuals, not decoration; not the same as "visual learners" |
| Concrete examples | Anchoring abstractions in specific cases | Moderate | Use several varied examples, then name the abstract principle, or learners tie it to surface features |
| Summarising | Writing summaries of material | Mixed | Helps when the learner is skilled at it; weak for many learners |
| Rereading, highlighting | Re-exposure to the text | Low utility as a primary method | Produces fluency (a feeling of knowing) rather than retrievable knowledge |

A frequently cited synthesis (Dunlosky and colleagues, 2013) rated practice
testing and distributed practice as high utility, several techniques as
moderate, and highlighting, rereading and summarisation as low; treat that as
a summary of evidence up to its date, not the last word (verify current
reviews if a learner asks for detail).

## Myths to decline, politely

- **Learning styles** ("I'm a visual learner, so teach me visually"):
  preferences are real; the claim that matching instruction to a preferred
  style improves learning has not held up when properly tested. Use the mode
  that suits the content (diagrams for structure, practice for skills) for
  everyone.
- **"We only use 10% of our brain"**, **left-brain / right-brain learners**:
  not supported.
- **Fixed retention curves with exact numbers** (for example a precise
  percentage remembered after a lecture versus teaching others): the
  "learning pyramid" figures have no traceable empirical source.
- **More hours always help**: unfocused hours and passive review add little;
  sleep and spacing matter.
- **Brain-training games improve general intelligence**: improvements tend to
  stay specific to the trained task.

When declining a myth, keep the useful part: "you like diagrams; we'll use
them where the material has structure, and add retrieval practice, because
that's what moves recall."

## Honest limits to state

- Effects are averages; individuals vary, and lab effects are often larger
  than classroom effects.
- Most evidence is about remembering and applying taught material. Evidence
  for creativity, deep expertise or motivation is thinner.
- Spacing and retrieval feel harder. Learners frequently judge them less
  effective while they are more effective; say so before they quit.
- Sleep, stress, health and time available dominate technique. A coach
  cannot fix those and should not pretend to.
- An AI coach can be wrong about content. Ground facts, and invite the
  learner to check anything that matters (exams, safety, money, health).
- No technique guarantees a grade. Do not promise one.

## Desirable difficulty

Some difficulty during practice improves long-term retention (spacing,
interleaving, generating before being shown). Difficulty the learner cannot
overcome is just failure: if success on retrieval is very low, shrink the
step, give a hint, or reteach. Aim for practice where the learner gets most
items with effort, not all of them instantly and not few of them.

## Metacognition

Learners are often poor judges of what they know (illusions of competence
from fluency). Build in calibration: before answering, the learner rates
confidence; afterwards, compare. Persistent overconfidence on a topic is a
signal to schedule more retrieval there.
